  <p><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="size-medium wp-image-25653 alignleft" src="https://encompassnw.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/img-blog-bb-01-300x300.jpg" alt="" width="300" height="300" srcset="https://encompassnw.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/img-blog-bb-01-300x300.jpg 300w, https://encompassnw.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/img-blog-bb-01-1024x1024.jpg 1024w, https://encompassnw.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/img-blog-bb-01-150x150.jpg 150w, https://encompassnw.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/img-blog-bb-01-768x768.jpg 768w, https://encompassnw.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/img-blog-bb-01.jpg 1080w" sizes="auto, (max-width: 300px) 100vw, 300px" />Over the past several weeks, families from across our community have been coming together—some joining live on Zoom, others watching recordings when life allowed—to explore a different way of understanding children, behavior, and even themselves. With 52 families participating in a mix of live virtual sessions and working at their own pace through recordings, <em>Balanced &amp; Brave: Brain-Based Parenting for Big Behaviors</em> has become a supportive space for parents and caregivers who want to move away from constant battles for control and toward parenting rooted in connection, compassion, and what we now know from neuroscience.</p>
  <p>At the heart of Balanced &amp; Brave is a simple truth: Children grow best when they feel safe, seen, and supported. When adults understand how the brain and nervous system really work, parenting becomes less about stopping outbursts and more about building relationships that help both parent and child feel connected.</p>
  <p><strong>Week 1: Felt Safety: Creating Emotional &amp; Sensory Security</strong></p>
  <p>We began the series by unpacking one essential concept: Felt Safety. It’s one thing for a child to <em>be</em> safe, but it’s another for them to <em>feel</em> safe in their body and nervous system.</p>
  <p>Families explored how tone of voice, posture, lighting, clutter, predictability, and even our facial expressions can send “<em>you’re safe</em>” or “<em>you’re not safe</em>” signals to a child’s brain. Instead of asking, “<em>Why are they acting like this?</em>” parents practiced shifting to, “<em>What is my child’s brain or body needing right now</em>?”</p>
  <p>We also talked about the power of co-regulation—how children borrow the calm of a connected adult thousands of times before they can access calm on their own. Parents tried out simple supportive phrases such as, “This is hard, and I’m right here with you.” Small sensory tweaks, cozy spaces, flexible seating, and visual simplification gave families practical tools to take home right away.</p>
  <p><strong><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="size-medium wp-image-25656 alignright" src="https://encompassnw.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/AdobeStock_610062006-300x200.jpg" alt="" width="300" height="200" srcset="https://encompassnw.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/AdobeStock_610062006-300x200.jpg 300w, https://encompassnw.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/AdobeStock_610062006-1024x683.jpg 1024w, https://encompassnw.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/AdobeStock_610062006-768x512.jpg 768w, https://encompassnw.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/AdobeStock_610062006-1536x1024.jpg 1536w, https://encompassnw.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/AdobeStock_610062006-2048x1365.jpg 2048w" sizes="auto, (max-width: 300px) 100vw, 300px" />Week 2: Sleep &amp; Rhythm: Supporting Rest Through Connection</strong></p>
  <p>Almost every parent has struggled with their child&#8217;s sleep routine at one time or another. Families learned to pay attention to their child’s natural sleep window, to create bedtime rituals that feel loving instead of rushed, and to use movement, humor, music, and progressive relaxation to help kids wind down.</p>
  <p>One of the most meaningful insights was that connection, not correction, is often what&#8217;s missing at bedtime. As one parent shared:</p>
  <p><strong><em>“Before, I felt like ‘What’s wrong with them?’ Now I’m wondering, ‘What’s going on for them?’ That shift alone has changed our evenings.”</em></strong></p>
  <p>Parents discovered that when adults slow their own pace, soften their voice, and make bedtime predictable, children settle much more easily. A regulated parent really does help create a regulated child.</p>
  <p><strong>Week 3: Temperament: Honoring the Unique Wiring of Each Child</strong></p>
  <p>Families were invited to look more deeply at temperament- the natural ways children experience the world from birth. We explored traits such as sensitivity, energy level, adaptability, intensity, and approach or withdrawal tendencies.</p>
  <p>Instead of thinking of these traits as “good” or “bad,” families learned to see them as valuable information and ask a new question: How do my temperament traits and my child’s traits work together or bump into each other?</p>
  <p>Through worksheets and conversations, parents mapped out both their own and their child’s temperament, noting where things flow smoothly and where friction tends to appear. The room was full of respect and relief. Temperament isn’t something to fix &#8211; it’s something to honor. Families shared strategies that made an immediate difference:</p>
  <ul>
  <li>Offering choices for kids who struggle with transitions</li>
  <li>Creating cozy, quiet spaces for slower-to-warm children</li>
  <li>Building movement breaks into the day for high-energy kids</li>
  <li>Using gentle warnings and predictable routines for kids who depend on structure</li>
  <li>Supporting sensitive kids with sensory-friendly environments</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Understanding temperament helped parents see their kids and themselves with so much more compassion.</p>
  <p><strong>Week 4: Co-Regulation → Self-Regulation — The Long-Term Vision</strong></p>
  <p>Our final session tied everything together by exploring how children develop self-regulation over time by being co-regulated thousands and thousands of times. In other words, co-regulation is the “training wheels” for emotional independence. Kids don’t learn to regulate simply by being told to “<em>calm down.</em>” They learn by watching safe adults:</p>
  <ul>
  <li>Name their feelings</li>
  <li>Notice body sensations</li>
  <li>Use breath, movement, or grounding tools</li>
  <li>Repair after ruptures or disagreements</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Parents practiced simple scripts such as:<br />
  <em>“I’m feeling overwhelmed, so I’m going to take three slow breaths before I respond.”</em></p>
  <p><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class=" wp-image-25561 alignleft" src="https://encompassnw.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Brave-300x200.jpg" alt="" width="275" height="183" srcset="https://encompassnw.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Brave-300x200.jpg 300w, https://encompassnw.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Brave-1024x683.jpg 1024w, https://encompassnw.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Brave-768x512.jpg 768w, https://encompassnw.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Brave-1536x1024.jpg 1536w, https://encompassnw.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Brave-2048x1365.jpg 2048w" sizes="auto, (max-width: 275px) 100vw, 275px" />Families then created their own Family Regulation Toolkits, filled with calming routines, sensory tools, emotional check-ins, and playful co-regulation strategies that fit their home and their child. We emphasized that regulation isn’t just a moment. It’s a rhythm created through repeated connection: shared breaths, inside jokes, rituals, movement breaks, or quiet presence.</p>
  <p>By the end of the night, families had both practical tools and a reassuring reminder: self-regulation is absorbed through connection, not taught through lectures.</p>
  <p>Throughout our time together, families consistently shared how transformative these small shifts felt.  They experienced less judgment and more curiosity, less tension and more connection.</p>
  <p><strong>Balanced &amp; Brave gently reminds caregivers:</strong></p>
  <ul>
  <li>Your child is not “too much.”</li>
  <li>You’re not doing it wrong.</li>
  <li>You’re doing something brave every time you choose connection over control.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>By understanding temperament, respecting sensory needs, prioritizing felt safety, and practicing co-regulation, families are building strong foundations for emotional health and resilience, now and into the future.</p>

Over the past weeks, 52 families participated in the "Balanced & Brave: Brain-Based Parenting for Big Behaviors" program, which focuses on fostering understanding and connection in parenting. The initiative, offered through live virtual sessions and recordings, emphasizes that children thrive when they feel safe, seen, and supported. Parents learned concepts like "Felt Safety," highlighting the importance of emotional security, and how to create calming environments through tone, posture, and predictability.

Subsequent sessions addressed crucial topics like sleep routines and children's unique temperaments, encouraging parents to shift their perspectives from correction to connection. By recognizing and honoring their children’s traits, families developed strategies to ease transitions and support sensory needs. The final session focused on the concept of co-regulation, illustrating how children learn self-regulation through observing and interacting with safe adults.

Participants reported transformative experiences, with a notable reduction in judgment and an increase in curiosity and connection. The program aims to build emotional health and resilience by prioritizing connection over control. A new series will begin in late January, with recordings of previous sessions available through the Encompass Online Learning System.
