Dopamine receptor inhibition plays a key role in both the benefits and drawbacks of antipsychotic medications, but the specific mechanisms affecting patients with schizophrenia are not fully clear. Hyperprolactinemia (HPRL), resulting from D2 dopamine receptor blockade, serves as a model to explore how genetic differences in glutamatergic signaling impact the effects of dopamine disruption. Researchers hypothesized that genetic variations in GRIN2A and GRIN2B, which encode NMDA glutamate receptor subunits, could influence the neuroendocrine effects of dopamine receptor inhibition. A study involving 536 schizophrenia patients measured prolactin levels and examined 23 GRIN2A/GRIN2B variants. The analysis found that genetic variations in NMDA receptors significantly affected the likelihood of developing HPRL, with the most pronounced impact linked to typical antipsychotics, which primarily block D2 receptors. This research provides clinical evidence that NMDA receptor genetic polymorphisms may predict individual responses to dopamine receptor blockade in schizophrenia. The findings suggest that relationships between dopamine and glutamate could also apply to other outcomes of antipsychotic treatment, including efficacy and side effects, highlighting the role of glutamatergic pathways in the effects of dopamine receptor targeting.
