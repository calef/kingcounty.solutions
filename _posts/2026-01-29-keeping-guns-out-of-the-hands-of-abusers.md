  <p>A landmark victory in Washington’s Court of Appeals has transformed the way trial courts must handle gun surrender in domestic violence cases.<strong> </strong>ELAP is proud to have partnered with the King County Bar Association (KCBA), Northwest Justice Project (NJP), and the Sexual Violence Law Center (SVLC) to secure the publication of <em>Dela Llana v. Holiwell</em>, making this critical ruling binding law across the state.</p>



  <h3><strong>The case: A pattern of defiance</strong></h3>



  <p>In early 2022, Grenadinah Dela Llana filed for a domestic violence protection order (DVPO) against Darrion Holiwell, a former King County Sheriff’s deputy and firearms instructor. The court issued a temporary protection order and an Order to Surrender and Prohibit Weapons, requiring Holiwell to immediately turn in all firearms.</p>



  <p>Holiwell claimed he had no weapons to surrender, despite records showing <strong>70 firearms</strong> registered to his name<sup></sup>. Over nearly two years and roughly 20 hearings, the trial court found Holiwell in persistent contempt<sup></sup>. He repeatedly offered “unpersuasive” and “not credible” explanations for the missing weapons, such as off-market sales without receipts<sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup>.</p>



  <p>Eventually, the trial court stopped requiring compliance hearings and instead issued a $100,000 fine<sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup>. Because Holiwell claimed to be indigent, the fine had little to no coercive power<sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup>. Effectively, the court had abandoned its duty to account for the missing weapons<sup></sup><sup></sup>.</p>



  <h3><strong>The ruling: A lifesaving mandate</strong></h3>



  <p>The Court of Appeals reversed the lower court’s decision, ruling that courts cannot simply give up when an abuser remains defiant<sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup>. The opinion emphasizes that:</p>



  <ul>
  <li>
  <strong>Courts have a statutory duty</strong> to verify “timely and complete compliance” with weapon surrender orders<sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup>.</li>



  <li>
  <strong>The burden of proof</strong> lies with the respondent (the abuser) to show, by a preponderance of the evidence, that they have complied<sup></sup>.</li>



  <li>
  <strong>Swift and certain compliance</strong> is mandatory to protect survivors and public health<sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup><sup></sup>.</li>
  </ul>



  <p>By granting the motion to publish this opinion, the court has ensured that its findings are now precedential. This means trial courts throughout Washington are now legally bound to use every tool available—including indefinite jail time for remedial contempt—to ensure abusers are disarmed.</p>



  <h3><strong>Why this matters</strong></h3>



  <p>Weapon surrender is a lifesaving public health tool. A survivor is <strong>11 times more likely to be killed</strong> when an abuser has access to a gun. For too long, “intractable behavior” from abusers allowed them to keep their weapons simply by exhausting the court’s patience<sup></sup>.</p>



  <p>This victory ensures that “I don’t know where they are” is no longer an acceptable excuse to end a court’s oversight. ELAP is honored to have stood alongside our partners in this fight to protect survivors and hold abusers accountable.</p>

A significant ruling from Washington’s Court of Appeals has changed how trial courts handle gun surrender in domestic violence cases. The case, Dela Llana v. Holiwell, involved Grenadinah Dela Llana seeking a domestic violence protection order against Darrion Holiwell, a former sheriff's deputy. Despite a court order for Holiwell to surrender his firearms, he claimed he had none, even though records indicated he owned 70 guns. Over two years, the trial court found Holiwell in contempt multiple times but struggled to enforce compliance. Eventually, the court issued a $100,000 fine, which had little effect due to Holiwell's claimed financial status.

The Court of Appeals reversed this decision, ruling that courts must ensure "timely and complete compliance" with weapon surrender orders. The burden is on the abuser to prove compliance, and courts can use strict measures, including jail time, to enforce these orders. This ruling establishes a precedent, emphasizing that survivors are at a significantly higher risk of harm when abusers have access to firearms. The ruling aims to enhance accountability for abusers and protect survivors, ensuring that excuses for non-compliance are no longer tolerated.
