feed_content: "<div>\n\n    \n    <p><img src=\"https://www.dav.org/wp-content/uploads/Hellfighters_article_Image3.jpg\">Even
  the blaring sounds of a 100-piece jazz band turned to whispers in the throngs of
  cheering Americans who lined New York City’s Fifth Avenue on Feb. 17, 1919.</p>\n<p>It
  was the city’s first homecoming for troops returning from the Great War. <a>The
  Sun newspaper</a> described the crowds as “two solid walls of booming, roaring welcomers,”
  and soldiers “decorated with flowers like brides.”</p>\n<p>The men were the city’s
  own, having originated as the 15th New York National Guard Regiment in Harlem before
  being shipped off to France as the 369th Infantry Regiment. They quickly gained
  a reputation for their steadfast fierceness in battle, eventually earning the moniker
  “the Harlem Hellfighters.”</p>\n<p>“Theirs is the finest of records,” the <a>New
  York Tribune</a> reported at the time. “Under fire 191 days, they never lost a prisoner
  or a foot of ground.”</p>\n<p>But their welcome was a far cry from the regiment’s
  sendoff in 1917 when they were prohibited from marching in the farewell parade of
  New York’s National Guard because of the color of their skin.</p>\n<p>It wasn’t
  the first and wouldn’t be the last battle the men of the 369th—composed entirely
  of Black soldiers under a white commander and mostly white officers—faced on the
  home front.</p>\n<p>They fought racist ideologies and policies to even be allowed
  to wear the uniform. Once in service, they were often overlooked, disrespected and
  undermined. Somehow, even sacrificing their bodies and contributing to the Allied
  victory still wasn’t enough to fully realize the rights, esteem and dream of American
  citizenship.</p>\n<p><strong>RED-BLOODED AMERICANS</strong></p>\n<p>Black Americans
  had served in uniform since the Revolutionary War, segregated from their white counterparts
  and often treated as inferior. Leading up to World War I, those circumstances hadn’t
  changed.</p>\n<p>Even in New York, a beacon for Black Americans escaping the post-Civil
  War South, resistance to Black people serving in uniform persisted. It wasn’t until
  1913 that legislation allowed for a Black unit in the New York National Guard—but
  victory was delayed.</p>\n<figure><img src=\"https://www.dav.org/wp-content/uploads/Hellfighters_Article_Image2.jpg\"><figcaption>Throngs
  of New Yorkers line Fifth Avenue to welcome home the soldiers of the 369th Infantry
  Regiment returning from World War I. But despite their valiant, patriotic service,
  members of the Black unit continued to face racism and discrimination at home.</figcaption></figure>\n<p>“It
  would be another three years before the actual organization of the 15th New York
  National Guard would take place because of the resistance of the white New York
  National Guard,” said Jeffrey Sammons, a professor emeritus of history at New York
  University and co-author of <a>“Harlem’s Rattlers and the Great War: The Undaunted
  369th Regiment and the African American Quest for Equality.”</a></p>\n<p>A series
  of events—including a 1916 fight on the Mexican border that depleted New York National
  Guard troops as World War I raged—finally led to the formation of a Black unit.</p>\n<p>“We
  need men in this war effort, and it doesn’t matter what color they are,” Sammons
  said of the sentiment at the time.</p>\n<p>Thousands of Black men answered the call.</p>\n<p>“An
  awful lot of them were patriotic and were red-blooded Americans who wanted to serve
  their country,” said Krewasky Salter, a retired Army colonel and military historian.
  “Their motivations for joining were analogous to just about any other American soldier.”</p>\n<p>But
  the Black service member who expected to prove his value and patriotism had a unique
  burden. That burden grew heavier on April 2, 1917, when then-President Woodrow Wilson
  declared in <a>his address to Congress</a> calling for war, “The world must be made
  safe for democracy.”</p>\n<p>Salter said that many Black soldiers thought, “If I
  continue to show my willingness to fight and die for this country, then perhaps
  I’m going to reap all of the benefits” of American democracy.</p>\n<p>By December
  1917, the 369th was in France.</p>\n<p><strong>A SLAP IN THE FACE</strong></p>\n<p>When
  Gina McVey recounts the story of the Hellfighters, she tells it like it’s old family
  lore—because it is. As a member of the 369th, her grandfather Lawrence McVey was
  among the first American infantry soldiers to arrive in France. But instead of rifles,
  McVey and his fellow soldiers were given pickaxes and shovels, Salter said. Although
  the regiment was all volunteer and infantry, it was designated as a draftee unit
  and given manual labor duties.</p>\n<p>“That was kind of a slap in the face,” Salter
  said.</p>\n<p>By early 1918, the 369th found their place in the war—under the French
  flag. Fighting since 1914, France’s forces were depleted. There was pressure for
  the U.S. to supply reinforcements to French command, but Army Gen. John Pershing,
  commander of the American Expeditionary Forces (AEF), had marching orders to maintain
  the independence and identity of American forces.</p>\n<p>“So the African American
  soldier gave Pershing an out, because he wasn’t going to give any of the white soldiers,”
  Salter said. “So he gave the African American soldier.”</p>\n<p>When Gina McVey
  started researching the 369th, she came upon a <a>now-infamous letter from AEF headquarters
  to French forces</a>. In it, the French are advised not to eat with or shake hands
  with Black soldiers and not to “commend too highly” the Black American troops. The
  danger, the letter stated, was that contact with the French—who didn’t hold the
  same racist ideologies held in America—would “inspire in Black Americans aspirations
  which to them [the whites] appear intolerable.”</p>\n<p>“It broke my heart,” McVey
  said of the letter.</p>\n<p>Under French command, the men of the 369th performed
  their duty, serving in the trenches longer and suffering more casualties than any
  other American regiment. They participated in important battles, including the deadly
  Meuse-Argonne Offensive, during which they captured the village of Séchault, and
  were there for the armistice of Nov. 11, 1918.</p>\n<p>Regiment soldier Henry Johnson
  became the first American ever awarded France’s Croix de Guerre military honor after
  engaging in hand-to-hand combat with an enemy raiding party.</p>\n<p>Lawrence McVey
  received the same French military honor for his courage in leading an attack that
  resulted in him being injured. According to <a>his application for veterans disability
  allowance</a> years later, McVey had suffered a gunshot wound to the arm.</p>\n<p>“I’m
  just so proud that he fulfilled his duties to the best of his ability, and they
  were instrumental in turning the tide of war,” Gina McVey said. “Knowing that—all
  the times people have told us we didn’t do anything and we were lazy and we were
  good for nothing—that we were good for something.”</p>\n<p>Another 169 members of
  the 369th received the prestigious Croix de Guerre. Yet at the time, few Black members
  received formal American honors.</p>\n<p><strong>HONOR DELAYED</strong></p>\n<p>Once
  the glory of their welcome home faded, the members of the 369th and other Black
  regiments were left to deal with racism intensified by the shifting roles brought
  on by the war.</p>\n<p>“One can see in the press that there’s a great deal of trepidation,
  of concern about these guys who have been trained to kill and had been transformed
  in many ways by their war experience and their interaction with people on a global
  stage,” Sammons said. “So it’s essentially, ‘how are we going to return Blacks to
  the status quo after the war?’”</p>\n<p>Within months of returning home, the country
  was seized by the <a>Red Summer of 1919</a>, an outbreak of racial violence across
  dozens of cities. Over the course of 10 months, an estimated 250 Black Americans
  were killed. In Arkansas, wounded 369th veteran <a>Leroy Johnston</a> and his three
  brothers were killed in the Elaine Massacre, the deadliest racial confrontation
  in that state’s history.</p>\n<p>It became clear that Wilson’s call to make the
  world safe for democracy didn’t apply to Black Americans, even those who fought
  in its name.</p>\n<p>Then in 1925, the <a>Army War College released a report</a>
  on the role and value of Black soldiers. In the report, Black Americans were described
  as “inherently weak in character” and accused of having “failed in the World War.”</p>\n<p>“This
  is a devastating document,” Sammons said, adding that it institutionalized the denigration
  of the Black soldier, something that continued into World War II. It wasn’t until
  1948 that then-President Harry Truman signed an <a>executive order</a> desegregating
  the U.S. military and mandating equality.</p>\n<figure><img src=\"https://www.dav.org/wp-content/uploads/GinaMcVey_Lawrence_Medal.jpg\"><figcaption>Lawrence
  McVey (middle), who served with the 369th, was honored with France’s Croix de Guerre
  after leading an attack that left him wounded. Thanks to the efforts of his granddaughter,
  Gina McVey (left), the medal now resides at the Smithsonian’s National Museum of
  African American History and Culture.</figcaption></figure>\n<p>For most of the
  369th’s Black members, it would take years, even decades, to receive American military
  honors. McVey received the Purple Heart in 1932. Johnson received the same in 1996,
  as well as the Medal of Honor in 2015.</p>\n<p>In late 2021, the 369th Infantry
  Regiment was awarded the <a>Congressional Gold Medal</a>—well after its members
  had died.</p>\n<p>“They need to be honored,” McVey said, “because they fought for
  a country that didn’t fight for them.”</p>\n<p>More than a century after the Harlem
  Hellfighters marched up Fifth Avenue to thunderous cheers, their story still resonates—proof
  that courage often arrives long before recognition.</p>\n<p>As the echoes of that
  parade fade, the nation can finally hear what was once drowned out: the call to
  honor all who fought for freedom.</p>\n\n</div><p>The post <a>For this Black World
  War I regiment, battle was on two fronts</a> appeared first on <a>DAV</a>.</p>"

title: For this Black World War I regiment, battle was on two fronts
topic_titles: []
---

On Feb. 17, 1919, New York City welcomed home the 369th Infantry Regiment, known as the Harlem Hellfighters, who fought bravely in World War I. Initially formed as the 15th New York National Guard Regiment, the all-Black unit faced discrimination, being barred from marching in their own sendoff parade in 1917 due to their race. Despite their valiant service, they encountered racism both during and after the war. Once deployed, they served under French command after being initially assigned menial labor, which was seen as a slight. The regiment excelled in combat, participating in major battles and earning several French military honors, while American recognition was delayed or denied.

Upon returning home, the soldiers faced intensified racism, culminating in the Red Summer of 1919, when racial violence surged across the U.S. The contributions of Black soldiers like the Harlem Hellfighters were often dismissed, as a 1925 Army report labeled them “inherently weak.” It wasn't until decades later, with the integration of the military in 1948, that their service began to be honored appropriately. The 369th Infantry Regiment received the Congressional Gold Medal in 2021, underscoring their long-overdue recognition.
