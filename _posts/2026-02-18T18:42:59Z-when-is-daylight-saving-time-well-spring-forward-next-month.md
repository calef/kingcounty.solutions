Daylight saving time will begin in the United States on March 8, 2026, at 2 a.m., when clocks will "spring forward" one hour, resulting in the loss of an hour of sleep. This biannual time change has been observed for over 50 years, with most Americans switching back to standard time on November 1. Despite ongoing public debate and legislative efforts, including the Sunshine Protection Act aimed at making daylight saving time permanent, no such law has been enacted. The bill, first introduced in 2018 by Florida lawmakers, has faced multiple rejections in Congress. Although it passed the Senate unanimously in March 2022, it failed to advance in the House.

Health experts advocate for permanent standard time, claiming it aligns better with biological rhythms and sunlight patterns. Daylight saving time was originally implemented during World War I by Germany to save energy and was formalized in the U.S. with the Standard Time Act of 1918. While Arizona and Hawaii do not observe daylight saving time, states may opt out of it under federal law. Historical attempts at year-round daylight saving time occurred during World War II and in 1974 during an energy crisis, but studies show minimal impact on energy consumption or crime rates.
