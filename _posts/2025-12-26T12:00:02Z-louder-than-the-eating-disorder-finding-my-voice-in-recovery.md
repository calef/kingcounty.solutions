  in Recovery **Content warning: This is one person’s story; everyone will have unique
  experiences in recovery and beyond. Some stories may mention eating disorder thoughts,
  behaviors, and symptoms. Please use your discretion when reading and speak with
  your support system as needed.Molly Morrison is a future physician, runner, and
  self-proclaimed journal enthusiast who is in ongoing recovery from anorexia and
  continues to choose healing each day. When she’s not writing or running, she’s probably
  curating a new recipe in the kitchen, laughing with her sisters, or planning her
  upcoming travels.This is a story of a time in my life when I stopped feeling, thinking,
  and existing fully as a human being. It is also a story of how I found myself again.For
  years, I underate and exercised my body into the ground. I thought that being “healthy”
  and “disciplined” would make me more lovable—not just to others, but to myself.
  Those thoughts came from everywhere: cultural standards, my social media feed, and
  comparison to others. I started to believe that the smaller I became, the more worth
  I had.There was nothing logical about my eating disorder, but at the time, it felt
  like the only thing giving me order and direction. I knew something was wrong—when
  I snapped at people I loved, when my body was giving me clear warning signs—but
  anorexia’s voice still drowned out my own.Recovery Isn’t What I ThoughtWhen my parents
  pushed me toward treatment, recovery began—though I didn’t know what that meant
  yet. I thought recovery meant getting back to “normal”—restoring weight, regaining
  a period, learning to eat again, and then forgetting about anorexia.But I quickly
  learned that healing from something that begins in your brain is very different
  from healing a broken bone. Recovery is not finite or linear. It’s not about erasing
  the voice of the disorder—it’s about learning to let your own voice become louder.And
  that’s where my journey began.Separating Yourself from the EDIn the early years,
  I shut myself off from the world. Eating disorders are isolating, and I thought
  I had to climb that mountain alone. At first, I didn’t even know how to explain
  the twisted pain inside my mind, so I didn’t try. I stayed quiet.But I started keeping
  a journal. At first, it was simply a private space to pour out thoughts I didn’t
  want anyone else to see. What I didn’t realize then was that those pages would become
  my lifeline. Through journaling, I began to see more clearly. I could separate myself
  from the eating disorder. I could look at my words on paper and say, “That’s Brittany’s
  voice, not mine.” (Brittany is the name I gave my ED—a way to externalize it, to
  see that its thoughts weren’t truly mine.) Writing gave me just enough distance
  to start reclaiming who I was, apart from the illness.But perhaps the most important
  shift journaling gave me was honesty. Anorexia thrives on lies: “You’re only valuable
  if you’re thin.” “You don’t need that meal.” “Rest means you’re weak.” My journal
  became the place where I could take those thoughts, name them for what they were,
  and rewrite them as truths.Here’s one example straight from my pages:What Brittany
  says is true: “Food is good or bad, and if I eat a ‘bad’ food, I am a bad person—unworthy
  of love from myself or others.”What is actually true: “Food is not morally good
  or bad. All food serves a purpose, and all food fuels me to live my life fully.”Side
  by side, the lie doesn’t look as powerful anymore. Putting it on paper exposes it
  for what it is: a distortion. And directly below it, you’re building a new foundation,
  truths rooted in compassion and self-trust.There is Power in CommunityEven with
  the progress I made through journaling, trying to recover alone eventually led me
  to relapse. I didn’t want to burden my family or friends. I doubted anyone could
  understand. Shame kept me quiet. But the truth is, eating disorders thrive in silence.
  The more I isolated, the stronger the disorder became. When I finally opened up—to
  friends, to providers, to others who had walked through similar battles—I realized
  something life-changing: I was not alone.Community didn’t “fix” me, but it gave
  me the courage to keep going. It reminded me that my experience was real and that
  I was worthy of being supported through it. Others held hope for me when I couldn’t
  hold it myself. And they also reminded me to keep practicing honesty—not only with
  them, but with myself. Because recovery requires honesty. It means naming the lies
  and speaking the truths out loud, even when it feels uncomfortable. It means admitting
  when you are struggling, instead of pretending you’re fine. It means trusting that
  people who love you can handle your honesty, and that you deserve to be loved in
  your truest form.I don’t believe I will ever completely unlearn the voice of anorexia.
  But I’ve learned how to respond to it. I’ve learned how to make my own voice louder,
  through journaling, through therapy, through community. Recovery is not a straight
  line, and I will probably always be “in recovery” in some sense. But the difference
  now is that I live with more freedom, more joy, and more honesty than I ever imagined
  possible.Take This With YouIf you are in the thick of an eating disorder or any
  similar struggle, I want you to take this with you:You are not your disorder. You
  are more than the thoughts in your head.Recovery is possible. It may not look like
  what you expect, but it will always be worth it.You do not have to do this alone.
  Journaling can help you find your voice, and community can help you amplify it.And
  above all, honesty is powerful (first admitting to yourself through journaling,
  and eventually to your support system). When you name the lies and rewrite them
  as truths, you begin to regain your strength and joy.Now, my voice is louder than
  Brittany’s. It leads me toward living my life fully instead of shrinking. And if
  you’re struggling too, I want you to know that you’re not alone. You don’t have
  to do recovery by yourself. There is hope, and there really is a full, beautiful
  life waiting for you beyond your eating disorder.We want to hear your voice of recovery!

Molly Morrison shares her journey of recovery from anorexia, emphasizing the importance of finding her voice and separating herself from the eating disorder, which she personifies as "Brittany." For years, Morrison battled feelings of worthlessness linked to societal standards of health and beauty, leading her to under-eat and over-exercise. Initially, she believed recovery meant merely restoring physical health, but she soon learned it was about amplifying her own voice over the disorder's negative messages.

Journaling became a critical tool for Morrison, allowing her to express her thoughts and recognize the lies perpetuated by anorexia. Through writing, she could distinguish her true self from the disorder, challenging harmful beliefs about food and self-worth. Despite making progress, she faced setbacks due to isolation and shame, but eventually found strength in community support and honesty. Engaging with friends and others who understood her struggles provided her with hope and encouragement.

Morrison concludes that recovery is an ongoing process and emphasizes that individuals are not defined by their disorders. She advocates for the power of community and self-expression in healing, reminding others that recovery is possible and worth pursuing.
