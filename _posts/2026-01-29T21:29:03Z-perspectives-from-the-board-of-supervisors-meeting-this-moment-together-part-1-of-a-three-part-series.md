source_text: By Steven Schauer, Deputy Executive Director At the King County Flood
  Control District, our mission is to implement flood risk reduction policies and
  projects to protect life and property while advancing equity and social justice,
  sustainable livelihoods, habitat protection, salmon recovery, recreation, and resilient
  communities. That mission is carried forward by a dedicated nine-member Board of
  Supervisors, each representing one of King County’s Council Districts and bringing
  deep knowledge of the communities they serve. For the second year in a row, we are
  proud to share a three-part blog series featuring perspectives from members of our
  Board. These reflections offer residents a closer look at how their local elected
  leaders are thinking about flood risk, recovery, and long-term resilience across
  King County. This first installment features messages from Vice Chair Sarah Perry
  (District 3) and Supervisor Jorge L. Barón (District 4). Their districts span urban
  neighborhoods, fast-growing suburban areas, and river valleys and foothills that
  experienced firsthand the impacts of December’s historic flooding. District 3 Statement
  from Sarah Perry, Vice Chair of the King County Flood Control District, representing
  King County Council District 3, including Carnation, Duvall, Fall City, Issaquah,
  North Bend, Redmond, Sammamish, Skykomish, Snoqualmie, Woodinville, and surrounding
  areas. December’s historic flooding will be remembered for generations in King County.
  The sheer scale and duration of the storm were extraordinary, and yet, we are profoundly
  grateful that there was no loss of life in King County. That outcome did not happen
  by chance. It reflects decades of investment in levees, floodplain management, warning
  systems, and partnerships that the King County Flood Control District and its local
  partners have built together. As we move from response into recovery, it is clear
  that our work is far from over. We now face the dual responsibility of addressing
  urgent short-term repairs to damaged flood facilities while also keeping our eyes
  on the long-term capital investments that will be needed to protect our communities
  in a changing climate. The year ahead will require careful prioritization, strong
  coordination, and a continued commitment to doing the hard work of rebuilding and
  improving our flood risk reduction system. At the same time, we must not lose sight
  of what makes this work truly meaningful. Flood Control District projects in District
  3, from the Snoqualmie Valley to its many tributaries, have shown that we can reduce
  flood risks while also advancing ecosystem restoration, salmon recovery, and other
  multi-benefit outcomes. As we implement the next generation of flood risk reduction
  projects, we will continue to pursue these shared benefits wherever feasible, ensuring
  that our investments support both thriving communities and a healthy natural environment.
  District 4 Statement from Jorge L. Barón, Supervisor for the King County Flood Control
  District, representing King County Council District 4, including much of Seattle,
  encompassing many of the communities from Belltown in the south to Broadview in
  the north, and west of I-5 to the water. We strive for equity to be at the heart
  of everything we do at the Flood Control District. That means ensuring our investments
  are balanced between rural river valleys and urban neighborhoods, and that communities
  facing very different types of flooding receive the support they need to stay safe.
  In District 4, flooding is driven primarily by stormwater and urban drainage issues,
  not large rivers. Heavy rain can overwhelm pipes, ditches, and creeks, flooding
  streets, homes, and businesses. That is why I encourage District 4 community organizations
  and local governments to apply for Flood Reduction Grants. These grants can fund
  projects that address urban flooding, from drainage improvements to green infrastructure,
  and help reduce flood risk right where people live and work. We also know that the
  best solutions protect more than just buildings. Our investments must help safeguard
  people, local property, and the natural environment together. Thoughtful flood risk
  reduction projects can improve water quality, create greener neighborhoods, and
  make communities more resilient to climate change. As we continue this work, I encourage
  everyone in District 4, and across King County, to visit the King County Flood Control
  District’s Flood Resources page and take steps to Be Flood Ready. Prepared communities
  are safer communities, and together we can reduce flood risk while building a stronger,
  more resilient future. More to Come in This Series This first installment highlights
  just a couple of the voices guiding the King County Flood Control District as our
  region recovers from a historic flood and looks ahead to a changing climate. Vice
  Chair Perry and Supervisor Barón bring distinct perspectives shaped by their communities,
  from urban creeks and stormwater systems to river valleys and regional infrastructure,
  but they share a common commitment to protecting people, property, and the natural
  systems that sustain us. In the coming months, we will continue this series with
  messages from additional members of the Board of Supervisors, offering even more
  insight into how flood risk reduction is being shaped across King County. Thank
  you for taking the time to learn more about the leadership behind the King County
  Flood Control District, and for being part of the collective effort to build a safer,
  more resilient future for all.

The King County Flood Control District aims to reduce flood risks while promoting equity, sustainable livelihoods, and environmental health. Its nine-member Board of Supervisors, representing diverse districts, shares insights in a new three-part blog series following last December's historic flooding, which notably caused no loss of life due to prior investments in flood management infrastructure. 

Vice Chair Sarah Perry emphasizes the need for ongoing recovery efforts, balancing immediate repairs with long-term capital investments to protect communities amid climate change. She highlights successful flood risk reduction projects that also support ecosystem restoration and salmon recovery.

Supervisor Jorge L. Barón stresses the importance of equitable investments in flood management, particularly in urban areas where drainage issues lead to flooding. He encourages local organizations to apply for Flood Reduction Grants to address these challenges effectively. Barón advocates for solutions that enhance water quality and community resilience.

The series will continue to showcase additional perspectives from Board members, reflecting on their commitment to safeguarding communities and natural environments while adapting to changing climate conditions. The Flood Control District encourages residents to engage with flood readiness resources for a safer future.
