  <p>By Steven Schauer, Deputy Executive Director</p>



  <p>This 2025 Year-End Review is being published in January 2026 following an extraordinary and historic flood event that impacted King County and much of the surrounding region in December. That flooding, lasting from December 8 through Christmas Day, required sustained emergency response, field monitoring, and infrastructure protection efforts, and understandably delayed the release of this annual reflection.</p>

  <p>The King County Flood Control District’s (KCFCD) Vision is: Building a future to withstand flood risks where thriving King County communities and healthy ecosystems coexist in harmony with area rivers.</p>



  <p>Our Mission is: Implementing flood risk reduction policies and projects to protect life and property while advancing various benefits including equity and social justice, sustainable livelihoods, habitat protection, salmon recovery, recreation, and resilient communities.</p>



  <p>Both our Vision and Mission were put to the test in real time during this unprecedented flood event. As we look back on the accomplishments of 2025, we do so with a clear understanding that flood risk reduction is not theoretical. It is immediate, dynamic, and essential to the safety and resilience of our region. The work accomplished in 2025, and in years past, helped communities withstand the momentous December flood event, and it continues to guide the KCFCD efforts as recovery moves forward.</p>

  <h2 class="wp-block-heading has-text-align-center"><strong>Preparedness and the Months Ahead</strong></h2>



  <p>Flooding remains the most common natural hazard in King County, and it can happen at any time during the year. The threat of flooding and any potential impacts and risks can never be fully eliminated, but it can be reduced by being proactive. For example, property owners and renters are encouraged to know about their flood risk and get flood insurance.</p>



  <div class="wp-block-media-text has-media-on-the-right is-stacked-on-mobile"><div class="wp-block-media-text__content">
  <p>Vice Chair Sarah Perry underscored the importance of the KCFCD’s work as the 2025-26 flood season, which runs from October through April, continues:</p>



  <p>“The infrastructure and habitat investments the Flood Control District has made in our rivers, streams, and other waterways in King County since its creation were vital during the historic flood event this past December, and they will continue to be vital in minimizing the impacts of flooding throughout this flood season and decades into the future.”</p>
  </div><figure class="wp-block-media-text__media"><img decoding="async" width="768" height="1024" src="https://kingcountyfloodcontrol.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/cm-perry-headshot-2025-768x1024.jpg" alt="" class="wp-image-7486 size-full" srcset="https://kingcountyfloodcontrol.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/cm-perry-headshot-2025-768x1024.jpg 768w, https://kingcountyfloodcontrol.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/cm-perry-headshot-2025-225x300.jpg 225w, https://kingcountyfloodcontrol.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/cm-perry-headshot-2025-1152x1536.jpg 1152w, https://kingcountyfloodcontrol.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/cm-perry-headshot-2025-1536x2048.jpg 1536w, https://kingcountyfloodcontrol.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/cm-perry-headshot-2025-100x133.jpg 100w, https://kingcountyfloodcontrol.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/cm-perry-headshot-2025-975x1300.jpg 975w, https://kingcountyfloodcontrol.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/cm-perry-headshot-2025-1384x1845.jpg 1384w, https://kingcountyfloodcontrol.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/cm-perry-headshot-2025-scaled.jpg 1920w" sizes="(max-width: 768px) 100vw, 768px" /></figure></div>



  <p>She also highlighted the KCFCD’s outreach efforts:</p>



  <p>“The King County Flood Control District will continue its community outreach, encouraging residents to visit our Flood Resources page at <a href="https://kingcountyfloodcontrol.org/flood-resources/">kingcountyfloodcontrol.org/flood-resources</a> to learn more about their flood risk and create emergency kits and evacuation routes. Everyone has a role to play in flood preparedness.   <h2 class="wp-block-heading has-text-align-center"><strong>Welcoming New Board Leadership in 2025</strong></h2>



  <p>Several leadership transitions occurred during 2025. In January, long-time District 5 Supervisor Dave Upthegrove became Washington State Public Lands Commissioner and was succeeded by De’Sean Quinn, who served the remainder of the term. In November, voters elected Steffanie Fain to represent District 5. District 2 Supervisor Girmay Zahilay was elected King County Executive and was replaced on the Board by Rhonda Lewis.</p>



  <p>Supervisor Lewis’s appointment marked two historic milestones for the KCFCD: she is the first Black woman to serve on the Board, and her appointment established the first majority-female Board in the KCFCD’s history.</p>

  <h2 class="wp-block-heading has-text-align-center"><strong>A Strong Financial Year and Forward Planning</strong></h2>



  <p>In May, the KCFCD received two clean audits from the Washington State Auditor’s Office, affirming strong fiscal stewardship and accountability.</p>



  <p>In November, the Board adopted the <a href="https://kingcountyfloodcontrol.org/king-county-flood-control-district-adopts-2026-budget-advancing-strategic-planning-and-flood-risk-reduction-efforts/">2026 budget totaling $114.1 million</a>, balancing near-term flood risk reduction needs with long-term planning while maintaining the same levy rate as 2025. Additional details about the 2026 budget will be shared in a forthcoming blog as the budget is likely to be amended in the weeks ahead to account for adjustments related to the historic flooding.</p>



  <h2 class="wp-block-heading has-text-align-center"><strong>Projects Completed and Emergency Response</strong></h2>



  <p>The KCFCD implements most of its flood risk reduction projects and programs through an interlocal agreement (ILA) with King County. In addition, the KCFCD also partners by ILA with local jurisdictions for flood protection projects and programs. Cities like Seattle, Bellevue, Kent, Renton, Black Diamond, North Bend, Snoqualmie, and many others can efficiently implement localized projects within their city limits. While King County does much of the work as a service provider to the KCFCD, this collaboration with all 40 jurisdictions throughout King County provides an effective method of implementing flood risk reduction projects across the county.</p>

The King County Flood Control District (KCFCD) published a year-end review for 2025, reflecting on significant achievements and challenges, particularly a historic flood from December 8 to Christmas Day. This event necessitated extensive emergency response and infrastructure protection efforts, underscoring the KCFCD's mission to reduce flood risk while promoting community resilience and ecological health. Despite the storm's severity, there were no reported fatalities in the county.

KCFCD leadership highlighted the importance of proactive flood risk management, emergency funding for repairs, and ongoing evaluation of flood mitigation infrastructure. In 2025, the district completed several flood risk reduction projects totaling over $3.7 million and distributed more than $35 million in grants to support various community initiatives and habitat restoration projects.

New leadership was introduced in 2025, with historic milestones including the first majority-female board. The district committed to expanding outreach and preparedness initiatives, particularly for vulnerable communities. Looking ahead, KCFCD aims to enhance infrastructure and community resilience while continuing to monitor and respond to flood risks throughout the ongoing flood season and beyond.
