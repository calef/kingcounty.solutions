  how much do we really understand? In this episode, Dr. Lexi Giblin, Executive Director
  of Opal, sits down with Meredith Blumenthal, a Registered Dietitian Nutritionist
  in private practice, to unpack the most common misconceptions surrounding GLP-1s
  and body weight. Together, they explore what these medications actually do, what
  they don’t do, and how changes in nutrition, metabolism, and food relationship occur
  for those taking GLP-1s.   Whether you’re a clinician, patient, or just curious,

  summary</strong> provided by Rev.com</p>\n<p>This podcast episode from “The Appetite”
  features Dr. Lexi Giblin interviewing dietitian Meredith Blumenthal about GLP-1
  medications and their use for weight loss.</p>\n<p><strong>What are GLP-1s?</strong><br>GLP-1s
  were originally developed to treat type 2 diabetes and other metabolic diseases,
  but were approved by the FDA for weight loss approximately four years ago<span> </span><a>[3:31]</a>.
  These injectable medications work through three mechanisms: they affect the brain,
  digestive tract, and various organs in the body<span> </span><a>[4:06]</a>. At higher
  doses, they target hunger, satiety, and appetite by essentially obliterating appetite,
  and they slow the rate that food leaves the stomach, causing gastroparesis<span> </span><a>[5:07]</a>.</p>\n<p><strong>How
  They Work and Concerns</strong><br>The slowing of digestion actually results in
  less effective breakdown and absorption of nutrients<span> </span><a>[5:49]</a>.
  While the medications reduce appetite, they don’t reduce the body’s nutritional
  needs<span> </span><a>[6:20]</a><span> </span><a>[6:43]</a>. This creates a concerning
  situation where people may experience nutrient deficiencies similar to those seen
  in severe anorexia, regardless of body weight<span> </span><a>[7:57]</a>.</p>\n<p><strong>Screening
  and Risk Factors</strong><br>The discussion reveals inadequate screening for eating
  disorders before prescribing GLP-1s<span> </span><a>[8:45]</a>. Many patients present
  with binge eating concerns, but providers often miss the role of restriction in
  binge-restrict cycles<span> </span><a>[9:19]</a><span> </span><a>[10:16]</a>. Weight
  stigma and lack of eating disorder training among prescribers contribute to this
  problem<span> </span><a>[11:07]</a>.</p>\n<p><strong>Long-term Use and Weight Cycling</strong><br>A
  critical finding is that GLP-1s must be taken long-term to maintain benefits. When
  people stop the medication, weight regain occurs almost immediately as appetite
  returns<span> </span><a>[15:40]</a><span> </span><a>[16:15]</a><span> </span><a>[17:05]</a>.
  The medications function similarly to dieting, and according to set point theory,
  may result in higher weights after discontinuation due to metabolic adaptations<span> </span><a>[24:07]</a><span> </span><a>[25:03]</a><span> </span><a>[25:31]</a>.</p>\n<p><strong>Muscle
  Loss and Harm Reduction</strong><br>GLP-1s can cause significant muscle wasting
  because muscle tissue is easier for the body to access for energy than fat, especially
  when protein intake is inadequate and physical activity is limited<span> </span><a>[26:37]</a>.
  For those choosing to use these medications, harm reduction strategies include regular
  DEXA scans to monitor muscle mass, ensuring adequate protein intake, engaging in
  resistance exercise, and structured eating schedules<span> </span><a>[28:07]</a><span> </span><a>[28:34]</a><span> </span><a>[29:49]</a>.</p>\n<p><strong>Social
  and Psychological Impact</strong><br>The medications affect not just nutrition but
  social and cultural aspects of eating. Blumenthal describes reading about someone
  constantly explaining their limited food intake at social gatherings by saying “I’m
  on a GLP-1,” noting this could easily be replaced with “I have an eating disorder”<span> </span><a>[35:43]</a><span> </span><a>[36:22]</a>.
  Food serves purposes beyond nutrition including tradition, culture, social connection,
  and celebration<span> </span><a>[36:39]</a>.</p>\n<p><strong>Safety and Accessibility
  Concerns</strong><br>The ease of accessing GLP-1s through online clinics raises
  safety concerns about counterfeit medications, lack of quality control, and improper
  dosing<span> </span><a>[40:57]</a><span> </span><a>[41:30]</a><span> </span><a>[42:00]</a>.
  The oral forms being advertised aren’t yet FDA-approved for weight loss<span> </span><a>[40:57]</a>.</p>\n<p><strong>Clinical
  Recommendations</strong><br>Both speakers emphasize the importance of body autonomy
  while ensuring informed consent. They recommend working with both dietitians and
  therapists, especially for those with eating disorder histories<span> </span><a>[30:43]</a><span> </span><a>[32:23]</a>.
  The goal is supporting individual decision-making while providing comprehensive

---

GLP-1 medications, initially developed for type 2 diabetes, have gained attention for their weight-loss capabilities. In a podcast episode, Dr. Lexi Giblin interviews dietitian Meredith Blumenthal to clarify misconceptions about these drugs. GLP-1s function by affecting hunger and satiety through interactions in the brain, digestive tract, and organs. While they can significantly reduce appetite, they may lead to nutrient deficiencies because they slow digestion without reducing the body’s nutritional needs. Inadequate screening for eating disorders before prescribing these medications is a concern, as many patients may have binge eating issues. Long-term use is essential to maintain weight loss, but discontinuing the medication often leads to rapid weight regain. GLP-1s can also cause muscle loss due to inadequate protein intake and limited physical activity. The episode highlights the social implications of using GLP-1s, as individuals may feel compelled to explain their eating habits. Safety issues arise with the easy access to these medications through online clinics, raising concerns about counterfeit products and improper dosing. Both speakers advocate for informed consent, emphasizing the need for collaborative care involving dietitians and therapists, especially for those with a history of eating disorders.
