  <p><em>We’re grateful to share that Youth Eastside Services (YES) was recently featured in <strong><span><span>The Seattle Times</span></span></strong> in an article highlighting the meaningful impact of mentoring relationships with young people. The story focuses on how small, consistent moments of connection between caring adults and youth can make a powerful difference, especially for young people navigating stress, change, and uncertainty.</em></p>
  <p><em>We’re thankful for the opportunity to be included in this coverage and to help elevate the importance of mentorship, connection, and showing up for youth in ways that truly matter.</em></p>
  <p><span><a>Read the complete article at <em>The Seattle Times.</em></a></span></p>

  <h1>Small moments make mentoring a young person special</h1>
  <p><span>By: Jayati Ramakrishnan</span></p>
  <p>[…]</p>
  <p>Iyla Mitteco remembers the first time she and her student mentee clicked.</p>
  <p>On one of their first outings, she picked up the seventh grader from school. As she got in the car, Mitteco asked her what she wanted to listen to.</p>
  <p>“She got so excited and said ‘Can we listen to Epic — The Musical?’ ” Mitteco said.</p>
  <p>The album, an adaptation of the Greek epic poem the “Odyssey,” wasn’t something Mitteco had heard of. But since her mentee asked, she decided to give it a try.</p>
  <p>She quickly realized it was something they both loved.</p>
  <p>Now, every time her mentee gets in the car, Mitteco said, they both know what’s coming through the speakers — and it’s “their thing.”</p>
  <p>“She’s very creative, and is somebody who really understands the creativity it takes to make something like that,” Mitteco said. “And it kind of sparks that creativity in me, too.”</p>
  <p>It seemed like a little thing to connect over, but for volunteers in the <span><a>SUCCESS mentorship program</a></span>, building a relationship with their young mentees is often about those small moments — going bowling, watching a favorite show or even just sharing a meal or taking a walk together.</p>
  <p>Run by the nonprofit Youth Eastside Services, the mentorship program has a straightforward mission: connect adult mentors with kids ages 6 to 18 who may need a supportive person in their lives.</p>
  <p>It’s one of several mental health services the nonprofit, one of 13 agencies supported by reader donations to <a><span>The Seattle Times Fund for Those in Need</span></a>, provides to youth across East King County. Others include in-school mental health staff members, counseling, substance-use treatment services and support for families. Recently, funding has been a challenge for the mentorship program, and the agency is seeking new donors to ensure its survival.</p>
  <p>For the mentors, it can be a bit daunting to start. They’re figuring out how to establish a bond and be helpful to a young person they’ve never met. But the rewards are innumerable: They say their mentees have taught them about the value of creativity and trying new things.</p>
  <p>“I just really love kids. They’re so funny and they love to learn and explore,” said Janet Morton, who mentors an eighth-grade girl from Bellevue. “I like that too, and it kind of helps me keep a little bit of kid in me.”</p>
  <p>Morton, a retired physical therapist who worked at Seattle Children’s hospital, said she became intrigued by the mentorship program after learning about it through her former job.</p>
  <p>“I’ve always felt it’s important to try and make an impact on the greater good of the world, and do something other than take care of myself,” she said. “What’s better than helping young people gain some self-confidence and tools to get through life?”</p>
  <p>Zander Norris, 25, said he was moved by the role the organization plays with local kids.</p>
  <p>Norris, who works in asset management at a Bellevue bank, found out about YES through a colleague who was on the organization’s board. He’s been a mentor to an eighth-grade boy since April.</p>
  <p>Norris said it’s important for volunteers to put themselves in their students’ shoes — to understand that it might initially be a little awkward for an elementary or middle schooler to talk to an adult stranger, and they may not open up right away.</p>
  <p>“There was certainly a little bit of a barrier at first,” said Norris, a Seattle resident. “It created a line of thought for me that I hadn’t interacted with a middle schooler in a long time.”</p>
  <p>Despite the initial adjustment period, Norris said he and his mentee have developed a rapport. The two go on walks or bike rides, play card games and go out for meals.</p>
  <p>“He’s smart as a whip, and has such a creative mind,” Norris said. “It just feels like a cool, friendly connection that I think we both benefit from.”</p>

Youth Eastside Services (YES) was featured in The Seattle Times for its mentorship program, which emphasizes the importance of small, consistent moments in building relationships between adults and youth. The article highlights how these connections can significantly impact young people facing stress and uncertainty. For example, mentor Iyla Mitteco and her seventh-grade mentee bonded over a shared love of "Epic — The Musical," illustrating how simple activities like listening to music can create lasting connections.

YES's SUCCESS mentorship program pairs adult volunteers with youth aged 6 to 18, aiming to provide support and guidance. Despite facing funding challenges, the program plays a crucial role in offering mental health services, including counseling and substance-use treatment.

Mentors like Janet Morton and Zander Norris share their experiences, emphasizing the joy and creativity they find in these relationships. Morton noted the importance of helping young people build self-confidence, while Norris reflected on the initial awkwardness of connecting with a middle schooler but ultimately found the experience rewarding. The program aims to continue fostering these meaningful connections for youth in East King County.
