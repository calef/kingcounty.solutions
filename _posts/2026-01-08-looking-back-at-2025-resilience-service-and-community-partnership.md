  <p>By Steven Schauer, Deputy Executive Director</p>



  <p>This 2025 Year-End Review is being published in January 2026 following an extraordinary and historic flood event that impacted King County and much of the surrounding region in December. That flooding, lasting from December 8 through Christmas Day, required sustained emergency response, field monitoring, and infrastructure protection efforts, and understandably delayed the release of this annual reflection.</p>


  <div>
  <figure><img src="https://kingcountyfloodcontrol.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Snoqulamie-River-flooding-12-11-25-1024x768.jpg"><figcaption>Snoqualmie River flooding near NE 124th St. Dec. 11, 2025</figcaption></figure>
  </div>


  <p>The King County Flood Control District’s (KCFCD) Vision is: Building a future to withstand flood risks where thriving King County communities and healthy ecosystems coexist in harmony with area rivers.</p>



  <p>Our Mission is: Implementing flood risk reduction policies and projects to protect life and property while advancing various benefits including equity and social justice, sustainable livelihoods, habitat protection, salmon recovery, recreation, and resilient communities.</p>



  <p>Both our Vision and Mission were put to the test in real time during this unprecedented flood event. As we look back on the accomplishments of 2025, we do so with a clear understanding that flood risk reduction is not theoretical. It is immediate, dynamic, and essential to the safety and resilience of our region. The work accomplished in 2025, and in years past, helped communities withstand the momentous December flood event, and it continues to guide the KCFCD efforts as recovery moves forward.</p>



  <h2><strong>Leadership Reflections Following Historic Flooding</strong></h2>



  <div>
  <figure><img src="https://kingcountyfloodcontrol.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Councilmember-Reagan-Dunn-Official-Portrait_600x900.jpg"></figure><div>
  <p>King County Flood Control District Chair Reagan Dunn reflected on the scale of the December flooding and the collective response across the county:</p>



  <p>“King County experienced an extraordinary flood event from December 8 through Christmas Day. Despite the severity and duration of the storm, we are deeply grateful that there was no loss of life in King County. The King County Flood Control District recognizes and thanks the first responders, field crews, engineers, and public works teams from King County, local jurisdictions, and federal partners who worked tirelessly, often in dangerous conditions, to monitor infrastructure and implement emergency measures during the flood.”</p>
  </div>
  </div>



  <p>Chair Dunn emphasized the KCFCD’s role during and after the event:</p>



  <p>“While the Flood Control District is not responsible for emergency response, we actively provided emergency funding to support urgent levee and revetment repairs during the event. As we collectively move into the recovery phase, the King County Flood Control District will remain deeply engaged in the weeks, months, and years ahead. This includes supporting immediate and short-term repairs to levees and revetments, evaluating the performance of flood mitigation facilities, and prioritizing long-term capital investments to further strengthen flood risk reduction throughout King County.”</p>


  <div>
  <figure><img src="https://kingcountyfloodcontrol.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/flood-patrol-member-taking-notes-while-patroling-Cedar-River-credit-KC.jpg"><figcaption>Flood patrol member taking notes while patrolling the  Cedar River during December 2025 flooding. Credit: King County.</figcaption></figure>
  </div>


  <h2><strong>Preparedness and the Months Ahead</strong></h2>



  <p>Flooding remains the most common natural hazard in King County, and it can happen at any time during the year. The threat of flooding and any potential impacts and risks can never be fully eliminated, but it can be reduced by being proactive. For example, property owners and renters are encouraged to know about their flood risk and get flood insurance.</p>



  <div>
  <div>
  <p>Vice Chair Sarah Perry underscored the importance of the KCFCD’s work as the 2025-26 flood season, which runs from October through April, continues:</p>



  <p>“The infrastructure and habitat investments the Flood Control District has made in our rivers, streams, and other waterways in King County since its creation were vital during the historic flood event this past December, and they will continue to be vital in minimizing the impacts of flooding throughout this flood season and decades into the future.”</p>
  </div>
  <figure><img src="https://kingcountyfloodcontrol.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/cm-perry-headshot-2025-768x1024.jpg"></figure>
  </div>



  <p>She also highlighted the KCFCD’s outreach efforts:</p>



  <p>“The King County Flood Control District will continue its community outreach, encouraging residents to visit our Flood Resources page at <a>kingcountyfloodcontrol.org/flood-resources</a> to learn more about their flood risk and create emergency kits and evacuation routes. Everyone has a role to play in flood preparedness. Knowing your local flood risk, signing up for flood alerts, and preparing for evacuation when necessary are simple but critical steps that can help you protect yourself, your family, your home, and your community.”</p>


  <div>
  <figure><img src="https://kingcountyfloodcontrol.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/BFR-banner.jpg"><figcaption>Be Flood Ready</figcaption></figure>

  <h2><strong>Welcoming New Board Leadership in 2025</strong></h2>



  <p>Several leadership transitions occurred during 2025. In January, long-time District 5 Supervisor Dave Upthegrove became Washington State Public Lands Commissioner and was succeeded by De’Sean Quinn, who served the remainder of the term. In November, voters elected Steffanie Fain to represent District 5. District 2 Supervisor Girmay Zahilay was elected King County Executive and was replaced on the Board by Rhonda Lewis.</p>



  <p>Supervisor Lewis’s appointment marked two historic milestones for the KCFCD: she is the first Black woman to serve on the Board, and her appointment established the first majority-female Board in the KCFCD’s history.</p>



  <figure>
  <figure><img src="https://kingcountyfloodcontrol.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Steffanie-Fain-credit-KC.jpg"><figcaption>Supervisor Steffanie Fain sworn in on Nov. 25, 2025. Credit: King County.</figcaption></figure>



  <figure><img src="https://kingcountyfloodcontrol.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Rhonda-Lewis-credit-king-county.jpg"><figcaption>Supervisor Rhonda Lewis sworn in on Dec. 9, 2025. Credit: King County.</figcaption></figure>
  </figure>



  <p>The KCFCD extends its appreciation to all outgoing and incoming Supervisors for their leadership during a demanding and consequential year.</p>



  <h2><strong>A Strong Financial Year and Forward Planning</strong></h2>



  <p>In May, the KCFCD received two clean audits from the Washington State Auditor’s Office, affirming strong fiscal stewardship and accountability.</p>



  <p>In November, the Board adopted the <a>2026 budget totaling $114.1 million</a>, balancing near-term flood risk reduction needs with long-term planning while maintaining the same levy rate as 2025. Additional details about the 2026 budget will be shared in a forthcoming blog as the budget is likely to be amended in the weeks ahead to account for adjustments related to the historic flooding.</p>



  <h2><strong>Projects Completed and Emergency Response</strong></h2>



  <p>The KCFCD implements most of its flood risk reduction projects and programs through an interlocal agreement (ILA) with King County. In addition, the KCFCD also partners by ILA with local jurisdictions for flood protection projects and programs. Cities like Seattle, Bellevue, Kent, Renton, Black Diamond, North Bend, Snoqualmie, and many others can efficiently implement localized projects within their city limits. While King County does much of the work as a service provider to the KCFCD, this collaboration with all 40 jurisdictions throughout King County provides an effective method of implementing flood risk reduction projects across the county.</p>



  <p>In 2025, the KCFCD completed three planned capital projects, totaling more than $3.7 million:</p>

  <h2><strong>Scientific Investments and Nationally Recognized Expertise</strong></h2>



  <p>In 2025, key studies were completed to guide future investments, including levee breach mapping for the <a>Raging</a> and Tolt rivers, the first-ever <a>Channel Migration Zone map for the lower Snoqualmie River</a>, and updated floodplain maps for Little Soos, Jenkins, and Covington creeks.</p>



  <p>In May, KCFCD and King County staff jointly hosted a peer agency from Portland to demonstrate integrated flood risk reduction and habitat restoration projects. KCFCD staff also shared expertise nationally through a flood warning systems webinar in October hosted by the National Association of Flood and Stormwater Management Agencies.</p>

  <h2><strong>Flood Event Reports</strong></h2>



  <p>River conditions are tracked in King County using a Flood Phase rating system ranging from Flood Phase 0 to Flood Phase 4. The KCFCD prepares Flood Event Reports following flood events that meet or exceed Flood Phase 2.</p>


  <div>
  <figure><img src="https://kingcountyfloodcontrol.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Flood-Phases.jpg"><figcaption>Flood Phase chart for rivers in King County</figcaption></figure>
  </div>


  <p>Earlier in 2025, Flood Phase 2 events occurred in <a>February</a>, <a>March</a>, <a>April</a>, and <a>November</a>.</p>



  <p>The KCFCD will soon publish a Flood Event Report documenting the historic December 2025  flooding. The report will provide a timeline of the flood event, which reached Flood Phase 4 in nearly every river basin in King County.</p>



  <figure>
  <figure><img src="https://kingcountyfloodcontrol.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Green-River-flooding-12-10-25-1024x768.jpg"><figcaption>Green River flooding. Dec. 10, 2025</figcaption></figure>

  <h2><strong>Be Flood Ready Outreach and Community Investment</strong></h2>



  <p>Following devastating floods elsewhere in the country earlier in the year, including the tragic July 4 flooding in Texas, the <a>Board unanimously directed staff to expand flood preparedness outreach</a>. In October, at the beginning of the 2025-2026 flood season, the KCFCD:</p>



  <ul>
  <li>Expanded the <a>Be Flood Ready brochure</a> from 4 to 8 pages</li>



  <li>Translated the brochure into 23 languages</li>



  <li>Produced the KCFCD’s first <a>Be Flood Ready video</a>
  </li>



  <li>Increased outreach to Duwamish River communities vulnerable to King Tides</li>
  </ul>


  <div>
  <figure><img src="https://kingcountyfloodcontrol.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Duwamish-King-Tides-postcard.jpg"><figcaption>Be Flood Ready Duwamish King Tides Postcard</figcaption></figure>

  <p>This year also marked 25 years of watershed-based salmon recovery for the Water Resource Inventory Areas (WRIAs). For over 12 years, the KCFCD has allocated a portion of its capital budget through the CWM Grant Program to fund salmon habitat protection and restoration and watershed health priorities, providing significant annual funding to the WRIAs in King County. The KCFCD’s long-term investments in the WRIAs now total:</p>



  <ul>
  <li><a>$17.2 million for WRIA 7</a></li>



  <li><a>$40 million for WRIA 8</a></li>



  <li><a>$36 million for WRIA 9</a></li>



  <li><a>Nearly $5 million for WRIA 10</a></li>

In January 2026, the King County Flood Control District (KCFCD) published its year-end review for 2025, reflecting on a historic flood that impacted the region from December 8 to Christmas Day. The flooding necessitated emergency responses and infrastructure protection efforts, highlighting the importance of KCFCD’s mission to reduce flood risks while promoting equity, habitat protection, and community resilience. Chair Reagan Dunn noted the absence of fatalities during the flood and praised the efforts of first responders and public works teams. The KCFCD actively provided emergency funding for levee repairs and is committed to ongoing recovery efforts.

In 2025, KCFCD completed over $3.7 million in flood risk reduction projects and distributed more than $35.6 million in grants for various initiatives. The organization’s outreach efforts expanded, promoting flood preparedness among residents. Leadership transitions included the election of new board members, marking significant milestones for diversity within the KCFCD.

Executive Director Michelle Clark commended the staff's dedication during the flood crisis. As the flood season continues, KCFCD emphasizes the importance of community preparedness and ongoing investment in flood risk reduction initiatives to protect lives and property in King County.
